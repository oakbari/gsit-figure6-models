# Figure 6 legend

Figure 6. Effects of male mating competitiveness, fertility and population growth on reproductive output and male ratios required for suppression. All parameter values are illustrative and were not fitted to the experimental ngSIT strains. Relative reproductive output is G = (1 + crf)/(1 + cr), where c is released-male mating competitiveness relative to wild type, r is the ratio of released to wild males available to mate, and f is viable-offspring production following a released-male mating relative to a wild-type mating. A ratio of 10:1 represents ten released males per wild male. (A) Can higher male ratios compensate for lower mating competitiveness? Reproductive-output reduction, 100(1 − G), across c = 0–1 and r = 0–50, assuming complete sterility (f = 0). Solid white contours mark 90% and 95% reduction. Increasing the male ratio compensates for reduced competitiveness when c > 0. (B) How does fertility limit reproductive suppression at a fixed male ratio? Reproductive-output reduction across c = 0–1 and f = 0–20%, at a male ratio of 10:1. Solid white contours mark 50% and 75% reduction. Higher relative fertility reduces the reproductive effect at a given competitiveness. Panels A and B share a linear percentage color scale; yellow indicates greater reproductive-output reduction. (C) What male ratio achieves 99% adult suppression when some released males are fully fertile? Maintained male ratio required for at least 99% equilibrium adult-female suppression across c = 0–1 and fully fertile males comprising 0–1% of the release cohort, assuming an illustrative low-density adult-daughter multiplier R₀ = 4. Here, f represents the fraction of fully fertile released males, with all remaining released males completely sterile. Fertile contaminants have wild-type fertility and are assumed equally competitive with sterile released males; under these assumptions, their numerical fraction equals mean relative fertility following a released-male mating. Dashed contours mark required ratios of 10:1 and 50:1. The outlined white point marks c = 0.5 and 1% fully fertile contamination, for which a maintained ratio of approximately 6.19:1 achieves the target. Across the displayed range, the required ratio depends more strongly on competitiveness than on contamination under these assumptions. The fertility feasibility boundary, approximately 25.19%, lies outside the displayed range. (D) How does population growth change the male ratio required for 99% adult suppression? Maintained male ratio required for the same equilibrium adult-female suppression target across c = 0–1 and R₀ = 2–10, assuming complete sterility. Dashed contours mark required ratios of 5:1, 10:1 and 20:1. Faster population growth increases the required ratio at a given competitiveness. Panels C and D share a logarithmic ratio color scale; blue/purple indicates lower required ratios and yellow indicates higher required ratios. Finite ratios at or below 1:1 and at or above 1,000:1 use the respective end colors; numerical grid values remain uncapped. Positive suppression targets cannot be achieved at c = 0. The mating model assumes random effective mating, one effective mating per female and equal female fecundity across mate classes. Population recruitment follows Fₜ₊₁ = R₀GFₜ/[1 + (R₀ − 1)GFₜ/K], where Fₜ is adult-female abundance and K is untreated equilibrium female abundance. Panels A–B describe reproductive output within one mating cohort; C–D describe deterministic population equilibria under maintained effective male ratios, constant parameters, no immigration and saturating density regulation acting on viable recruitment. Fertile contamination of the final release cohort is distinct from the parental sorting-error rate; their relationship depends on the crossing configuration, offspring genotypes and downstream quality control. The modeled requirements do not establish extinction probability, time to suppression, field efficacy, release frequency or production throughput.
